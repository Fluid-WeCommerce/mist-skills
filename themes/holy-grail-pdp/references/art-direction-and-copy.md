## 1. Creative north star

Choose one visual thesis that the product can own. It must grow from a real product truth, buyer ritual, material behavior, or brand asset—not from a generic design trend.

Useful thesis sources:

- product material, texture, ingredient, construction, or transformation;
- a recognizable moment of use;
- a distinctive crop, grid, frame, line, or compositional rule;
- a motion behavior that explains rather than decorates;
- a brand symbol or packaging behavior extended into layout.

Write the thesis in one sentence: `Use <device> to make <product truth or buyer outcome> tangible.` If the sentence could describe any competitor, sharpen it.

Apply the thesis hardest to three moments:

1. the hero;
2. the mechanism/explanation;
3. the strongest proof story.

Let utility sections become quieter. Constant spectacle destroys hierarchy.

## 2. Page rhythm

Alternate jobs, density, and media behavior:

- **Decision:** compact product facts, choices, terms, and CTA.
- **Desire:** large visual, transformation, ritual, or sensory moment.
- **Explanation:** diagram, sequence, comparison, or labeled detail.
- **Proof:** review, demonstration, method, expert, certification, or policy.
- **Breath:** short connective copy or restrained whitespace.

Avoid more than two consecutive sections with the same layout grammar. A page of identical icon cards communicates that every point matters equally.

Use visual scale to express claim priority:

- one primary outcome;
- two or three supporting benefits;
- details and objections at lower emphasis;
- legal/method context always readable, never hidden through tiny type.

## 3. Hero contract

The first decision view must answer:

- What is this?
- Is it the product/promise I clicked?
- What meaningful outcome or use is relevant?
- What does it cost?
- Which choices must I make?
- Why is the next action safe?

Recommended hierarchy:

1. optional factual eyebrow;
2. concrete outcome headline;
3. one-sentence reason to believe;
4. rating/proof with context;
5. total price and honest savings basis;
6. variant, quantity, and purchase mode;
7. delivery/risk line;
8. state-aware CTA.

The hero media must do one dominant job: identify the product, demonstrate use, reveal material/sensory value, or mirror the acquisition creative. Do not make one asset perform all four jobs.

For copy, prefer concrete nouns and observable verbs. Replace abstract claims such as `elevate`, `revolutionize`, `premium`, or `game-changing` with a real outcome, mechanism, material, ritual, time, or constraint.

## 4. Mechanism and proof

### Mechanism

Make the causal story retellable in one breath:

`input or design choice → verified action → buyer-relevant consequence`

Use labeled diagrams, sequence, macro detail, or controlled demonstration. Do not use molecular imagery, lab coats, technical grids, or percentages as decorative science theater.

### Proof choreography

Use the strongest appropriate proof for the claim risk:

1. product fact or demonstrated behavior;
2. transparent customer context and verified review;
3. documented testing with population/method/duration/source;
4. exact certification or expert scope;
5. fair like-for-like comparison.

Keep disclosures adjacent. Let raw UGC preserve human texture; use controlled brand media to explain. Do not polish both into the same voice.

### Comparison

Compare the factors buyers truly weigh: outcome, effort, ingredients/materials, evidence, fit, service, and total value. Include a `best for` row and allow an alternative to win where it genuinely serves another need.

## 5. Copy hierarchy

For every section write:

- **Buyer question:** the uncertainty being resolved.
- **Message:** one sentence the shopper should remember.
- **Proof:** the exact supporting fact or source.
- **Headline:** the message made memorable without overstating it.
- **Body:** only the context necessary to understand or believe it.
- **Transition:** why the next section follows.

Keep claims and proof close enough to scan together. Do not place a strong promise in a heading and its limitation several screens away.

Use microcopy where uncertainty occurs:

- variant availability beside variants;
- renewal/cancellation beside subscription choice;
- delivery timing beside purchase action;
- guarantee eligibility beside the guarantee;
- size/fit/material care beside configuration;
- incentive disclosure beside review/UGC.

## 6. Mobile art direction

At 390px:

- select one 4:5 or 9:16 dominant media asset before the purchase controls;
- turn secondary media into a swipe rail with a visible count/state;
- use full-width content selectively; preserve side gutters for reading sections;
- make option controls large enough to identify state without color alone;
- compress dense comparisons into horizontally scrollable labeled regions or an accessible stacked form;
- limit sticky purchase UI to the minimum state needed now;
- reserve the bottom safe area and prevent chat/cookie/promo collisions;
- use motion to show change or state, and honor reduced motion.

Do not reorder mobile content randomly. Preserve the buyer-question spine even when sections change form.

## 7. Anti-patterns

Reject or repair:

- generic centered hero followed by an undifferentiated card grid;
- ad-to-page creative or promise mismatch;
- headline claims with no nearby substantiation;
- logo walls with no relevance or source;
- review carousels containing interchangeable praise;
- equal visual weight for primary and minor benefits;
- hidden subscription defaults, savings math, renewal, or cancellation;
- fake countdowns, inventory, viewers, or urgency;
- sticky CTA that loses selection or covers support/consent controls;
- static price/variant markup that can disagree with Fluid product state;
- copied competitor imagery, copy, or trade dress;
- desktop modules simply stacked into a 10,000px mobile page;
- decorative animation that delays comprehension or interaction.
